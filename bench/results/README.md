# Results

Two rounds, and the second exists because the first answered the wrong question.

- **Round 2, [`COST.md`](COST.md)** — thirteen EC2 machines, **cost per proof** as the
  primary axis, latency alongside it. Generated by `bench/scripts/cost.py` from
  [`sweep/`](sweep/). Read this one first.
- **Round 1, below** — two machines against rapidsnark, latency only. Still valid as
  measured; its headline ratio is not a hardware result and is corrected here.

## Why there is a round 2

Round 1's headline was that the CUDA backend is 10.34x rapidsnark warm at 140,261
constraints on a g4dn.2xlarge, against 4.31x for Metal on an M2 Max. Both numbers are
correctly measured. The comparison between them is close to meaningless, for four reasons
that round 2 removes one at a time.

**The divisor was not the same quantity.** rapidsnark takes 554 ms on the M2 Max and
1579 ms on the g4dn's Xeon for identical work, so the GPU multiplier is larger on EC2
because the denominator is worse there. In absolute milliseconds the Mac's GPU was the
faster of the two at every size above the smallest. A speedup over a CPU baseline mostly
measures the baseline.

**The CPU baseline was doubly confounded.** rapidsnark ships hand-written x86_64 assembly
for `Fr` and `Fq` with no aarch64 counterpart, so on Apple silicon both provers fall back
to compiler-generated field arithmetic and on the Xeon only one of them does. Our prover
measured 0.92-1.19x of rapidsnark on the Mac and 1.35-1.49x *slower* on the Xeon. That was
attributed to the assembly, but it was an inference from two machines that differed in four
other ways, including 12 physical cores against 4. Round 2 settles it on a single box by
building rapidsnark twice from one source tree, `USE_ASM=YES` and `USE_ASM=NO`, **and the
attribution turns out to be wrong**: the assembly is worth 2.52x to rapidsnark, but with it
switched on our prover still lands within 3% of it on an AMD box, so it cannot be what made
us slower on the Xeon.

**One GPU SKU is not a GPU result.** A 2018 Turing T4 was the only NVIDIA part measured.
Round 2 spans four generations: Turing (T4, T4G), Ampere (A10G) and Ada (L4, L40S).

**Instance-to-instance variance was never checked**, so a difference between two EC2 runs
could not be told apart from a difference between two machines. Round 2 measures it.

## What round 2 changes

- **Our prover against our prover.** rapidsnark is not in the sweep, because its assembly
  was the confound. The primary comparison is the same Rust from the same commit on every
  machine. rapidsnark returns only in the isolated `USE_ASM` experiment.
- **Cost is the primary axis**, latency second. They do not have the same winner.
- **Prices are AWS's published on-demand feed**, cross-checked against an independent
  source; `pricing:GetProducts` is denied to this IAM user, so nothing is from memory.
  Spot is carried alongside because nobody runs a batch prover on-demand.
- **Every machine passes the full test suite before it may contribute a timing**: 125 tests
  across 39 suites, on two instruction sets and four NVIDIA architectures. A box that fails
  the gate publishes nothing. This is roughly 40% of each box's billed time and it is the
  reason the numbers can be put next to each other at all.

## The answer

At 140,261 constraints, warm, on-demand:

| | machine | accelerator | ms / proof | $ / 1000 proofs |
|---|---|---|---:|---:|
| **cheapest** | `g6.xlarge` | NVIDIA L4 (Ada) | 77.3 | **$0.017** |
| **fastest** | `g6e.xlarge` | NVIDIA L40S (Ada) | **66.0** | $0.034 |
| best CPU | `c8g.xlarge` | Graviton4 | 1,826.8 | $0.081 |

**The GPU wins the cost axis, not just the latency axis, and that was not the expected
result.** The best CPU machine rents for a fifth of the L4 box's hourly price and still
costs 4.7x more per proof, because the L4 finishes 24x sooner. Renting a more expensive
machine for a twenty-fourth of the time is cheaper, and no amount of picking the right CPU
closes that gap.

The CPU findings from the first half of the sweep survive intact and are now visibly second
order. Scaling efficiency falls from 100% at 4 cores to 96% at 8 and 87% at 16 within the
c7a family (91% for c8g at 16), and AWS charges strictly linearly per core, so a small box
is a few percent cheaper per proof than a large one in the same family. That is a real
effect worth a few percent, sitting inside an effect worth 4.7x.

Three things only a wide matrix shows:

- **Spot reorders it.** The discount is not uniform: 65% on the T4G, 53% on the A10G, 22%
  on the Ada cards. At spot prices the cheapest machine is `g5g.xlarge`, a T4G on a
  Graviton2 host, at **$0.007 per 1000 proofs** -- roughly half the L4's spot price and a
  twelfth of the best CPU's.
- **Circuit size changes the winner.** Below about 20k constraints the T4G is cheapest
  on-demand too, because the faster Ada cards cannot amortise their fixed per-proof cost
  over a small circuit.
- **Renting a card and not using it is the most expensive thing here.** Running the CPU
  backend on the GPU boxes costs $0.43 to $1.40 per 1000 proofs, up to 80x the cheapest
  option and 17x the best dedicated CPU box.

**What to actually run.** For a saturated prover, a current-generation GPU, and the mid-tier
one rather than the top: the L40S is 17% faster than the L4 and exactly twice the cost per
proof. If the workload tolerates interruption, the T4G on spot is cheaper again by a factor
of two. Choose a CPU box only when no GPU is available, and then Graviton4 at the smallest
size that fits -- the whole 140k-constraint proof peaks at 215 MB, so memory never forces a
larger instance.

## rapidsnark's assembly, settled on one box, and a Round 1 claim withdrawn

Round 1 observed that our CPU prover ran at 0.92-1.19x of rapidsnark on the M2 Max but
1.35-1.49x *slower* on the g4dn's Xeon, and attributed the difference to rapidsnark's
hand-written x86_64 assembly for `Fr` and `Fq`, which has no aarch64 counterpart. That was
an inference from two machines that differed in four other ways.

rapidsnark ships two build targets that differ in exactly one cmake flag, so the question
can be answered on a single machine, in a single run, with the same compiler and the same
source tree: `make host` (`USE_ASM=YES`) against `make host_noasm` (`USE_ASM=NO`).

On `c7a.2xlarge`, AMD EPYC 9R14, 8 cores, warm:

| circuit | rapidsnark, asm | rapidsnark, no asm | ours | what the asm buys |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| js_2x2_d32 | 101.0 ms | 276.0 ms | — | 2.73x |
| js_8x8_d32 | 432.0 ms | 1,145.0 ms | — | 2.65x |
| js_16x16_d32 | 832.0 ms | 2,099.5 ms | 856.9 ms | 2.52x |

**Half the Round 1 claim is confirmed and half is withdrawn.**

Confirmed: the assembly is enormous, worth **2.52x to 2.73x**. Against a rapidsnark built
from the same source without it, our Rust prover is **2.45x faster**.

Withdrawn: the assembly is *not* why we trailed on the Xeon. On this box the assembly is
fully enabled and our prover still lands within **3%** of it (856.9 ms against 832.0 ms).
If the assembly were the explanation, we would trail here too, and we do not. The Round 1
gap was specific to that Xeon -- a Skylake-SP 8259CL with 4 physical cores -- and not a
property of x86 or of rapidsnark's build. The honest summary is that rapidsnark's assembly
is what makes rapidsnark competitive with us, rather than what makes us slower than it.

## The two rounds do not define "cold" the same way

Worth knowing before comparing a number here against a number in Round 1, because the same
word means two different things.

Round 1's cold spawned a fresh **process** per rep and timed the whole `g16 prove`
invocation: process start, dynamic linking, key parse, prove, JSON write, exit. Round 2's
cold rebuilds the backend and reparses the key per rep **inside one process**. On the same
instance type, at 140,261 constraints:

| | round 1 cold | round 2 cold | difference |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| ours/cpu | 2301.2 ms | 2109.8 ms | 191 ms |
| ours/cuda | 695.6 ms | 473.2 ms | 222 ms |

The gap is about 200 ms on both backends, so it is process startup rather than anything
CUDA-specific. Neither definition is wrong: the first is what a CLI invocation costs, the
second is what a worker that re-prepares per job costs. They are just not the same
measurement, and quoting one against the other would invent a 1.5x improvement that does
not exist.

The warm numbers give a second, sharper reading of the same pair of runs. Between the two
rounds, on the same instance type, **CUDA warm differed by 0.9 ms (0.6%) and CPU warm by
118 ms (5.4%)**. The GPU is a passthrough device; the host CPU shares a socket with
whatever else the hypervisor put there. Instance-to-instance variance is real, it is
roughly six times larger on the CPU side, and Round 1 never measured it. Two separate
`c7a.xlarge` instances launched forty minutes apart agreed to 0.6%.

## What the cost model does not include

- Cost per proof assumes the machine proves **continuously**. Idle time is not modelled and
  would raise every row; a bursty workload also pays for boot, and a GPU box pays its kernel
  build again on every new instance.
- On a GPU box the host CPU is nearly idle and is being paid for. Co-scheduling CPU proofs
  alongside GPU proofs would raise throughput per dollar. That is not measured, so **every
  GPU row is pessimistic by an unknown amount**.
- **Spot prices move.** The snapshot here is one sample, and the discount was not uniform
  across families, which is enough to reorder the ranking. Re-sample before quoting.
- The M2 Max is not rented and has no hourly rate. Its cost column is imputed capex at 100%
  utilisation and is **not the same kind of number** as an EC2 rate. Metal cannot be priced
  on AWS at all: `mac2.metal` requires a 24-hour minimum dedicated-host allocation, so the
  cheapest possible Metal experiment on AWS costs more than this entire sweep.

# Round 1

Two machines, kept strictly separate. One results file per machine, every row stamped with
the machine and the accelerator, generated tables in [`TABLES.md`](TABLES.md), device-level
measurements in [`device-microbench.md`](device-microbench.md).

- `comparison-Apple-M2-Max.csv` — MacBook Pro (Mac14,6), M2 Max, 8P + 4E cores, 38-core GPU
- `comparison-aws-g4dn.2xlarge-Tesla-T4.csv` — EC2 g4dn.2xlarge, 8 vCPU Xeon, Tesla T4 (sm_75)

**Never read a row from one file next to a row from the other as if they were the same
experiment.** They are different CPUs, different GPUs and different core counts, and the
single most misleading thing in this directory is the temptation to do exactly that. See
"the ratio trap" below.

## The payload

Not a synthetic benchmark. A Zcash-style joinsplit: `N` inputs, `M` outputs, Poseidon Merkle
inclusion proofs of depth `D`, compiled with circom 2.1.4 against circomlib. Every variant
proves a real statement with a real witness generated by the circuit's own WASM.

| variant | constraints | inputs | outputs | depth |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| tiny_mul | 2 | — | — | — |
| js_1x1_d8 | 3,359 | 1 | 1 | 8 |
| js_2x2_d16 | 10,153 | 2 | 2 | 16 |
| js_2x2_d32 | 17,929 | 2 | 2 | 32 |
| js_8x8_d32 | 70,357 | 8 | 8 | 32 |
| js_16x16_d32 | 140,261 | 16 | 16 | 32 |

`tiny_mul` is a single multiplication constraint. It is there to expose fixed costs, not to
represent anything.

## Method

10 reps, medians with min and max. Medians rather than means because these runs are short
enough that one scheduler hiccup is likely, and it moves a mean but not a median.

**Every timing belongs to a proof that was verified before it was recorded.** A benchmark
that times a broken prover is worse than no benchmark. Verification is by rapidsnark's C++
verifier in the loop, plus our own verifier and `snarkjs groth16 verify` as cross-checks
where installed. On the EC2 box snarkjs was not installed, so the encoding cross-check there
reads `unknown` rather than `no`; the same artifacts pass it on the Mac, and the encoding is
machine-independent.

**cold** — one fresh process per proof. Key parse, kernel load and upload are all inside the
timed region. This is what a CLI actually does.

**warm** — setup paid once, then prove in a loop. This is what a resident service sees, and
it is the only mode in which a GPU backend can look its best, which is precisely why vendor
charts prefer it.

Neither backend has a size gate that would quietly fall back to the CPU on small inputs.
`--backend cuda` reporting a CPU number would make the whole exercise a lie.

Warm, at 140,261 constraints, against rapidsnark on the same machine:

| machine | rapidsnark | ours CPU | ours GPU | GPU speedup |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Apple M2 Max | 554.5 ms | 614.4 ms | **128.8 ms** (Metal) | 4.31x |
| EC2 g4dn.2xlarge | 1579.0 ms | 2192.0 ms | **152.7 ms** (CUDA) | **10.34x** |

Cold, same circuit:

| machine | rapidsnark | ours CPU | ours GPU | GPU speedup |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Apple M2 Max | 558.2 ms | 650.0 ms | **228.5 ms** (Metal) | 2.44x |
| EC2 g4dn.2xlarge | 1557.3 ms | 2301.2 ms | **695.6 ms** (CUDA) | 2.24x |

## The ratio trap, which is the most important thing on this page

**That 10.34x is not the T4 being twice as good as the M2 Max's GPU. It is mostly the Xeon
being bad.** Put the two GPUs side by side in absolute milliseconds, warm:

| circuit | M2 Max Metal | T4 CUDA | CUDA / Metal |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| js_1x1_d8 | 18.9 | **16.7** | 0.88x |
| js_2x2_d16 | **22.1** | 23.7 | 1.07x |
| js_2x2_d32 | **26.8** | 29.5 | 1.10x |
| js_8x8_d32 | **74.1** | 89.7 | 1.21x |
| js_16x16_d32 | **128.8** | 152.7 | 1.19x |

The M2 Max's GPU is *faster* than the T4 at every size above the smallest, by about 20%. The
speedup multiplier is larger on EC2 because the baseline it divides by is worse: rapidsnark
takes 554 ms on the Mac and 1579 ms on the Xeon for identical work.

The same effect shows in our CPU backend, and it is worth stating because it cuts against us.
Our CPU prover is within 0.92x to 1.19x of rapidsnark on the M2 Max, and 1.35x to 1.49x
*slower* on the Xeon. Same Rust, same arkworks. Anyone quoting a single-machine CPU
comparison as a statement about a prover is quoting a statement about the host.

> **Corrected by Round 2.** The sentence that stood here blamed rapidsnark's hand-written
> x86_64 assembly, on the reasoning that Apple silicon has no such path so both provers fall
> back to compiler-generated arithmetic there. Round 2 tested that directly, by building
> rapidsnark twice on one AMD box from one source tree with `USE_ASM=YES` and `USE_ASM=NO`.
> The assembly is worth 2.52x to rapidsnark, but with it fully enabled our prover still lands
> within 3% of rapidsnark on that box. So the assembly is not what made us slower on the
> Xeon; that gap was specific to the 8259CL. See "rapidsnark's assembly, settled on one box"
> above.

## Where the time goes, and three predictions that were wrong

Warm, 140,261 constraints, microseconds:

| stage | M2 Max Metal | T4 CUDA |
|---|---:|---:|
| gather | 138 | 2,558 |
| NTT (six transforms) | 7,294 | **5,964** |
| MSM (five) | **120,652** | 137,266 |
| assemble | 523 | 969 |

From the device microbenchmarks the T4 has 2x the field-multiply throughput and 60% of the
bandwidth of the M2 Max, so the predictions were: MSM faster on the T4, NTT slower. **Both
were wrong, in opposite directions.**

- **The NTT is faster on the T4 despite less bandwidth**, so it is not bandwidth bound in
  either implementation. The M2 Max streams at about 30 GB/s against its own 283 GB/s roof.
  The limit is the access pattern, not the bus.
- **The MSM is slower on the T4 despite twice the multiply rate**, so it is not multiply
  bound either. It is 92% of the proof, it is the least tuned part of the CUDA backend, and a
  forced window width `c=8` already beats the heuristic by 9% at this size. That is where the
  next work goes.
- **gather is 18x slower on the T4**, and that one was predictable: Apple's unified memory
  makes the "upload" a memcpy into a buffer the GPU already sees, while every witness on a
  discrete card crosses PCIe on every proof.

## Cold mode and the five-minute first run

CUDA's cold column carries a floor of about 310 ms that Metal does not have: loading 30.8 MB
of PTX and letting the driver JIT it. That is why cold CUDA is 404 ms even on a 2-constraint
circuit, and why the cold crossover against rapidsnark sits between 18K and 70K constraints
while the warm crossover is around 3.4K.

Worse, that 310 ms is itself the *warm* figure. The genuine first run of the CUDA backend on
a fresh machine costs **270 to 300 seconds**: 113.6 s of NVRTC and about 175 s of driver JIT,
both cached afterwards in `~/.nv/ComputeCache`. Deleting that directory reproduces both.

The cold numbers above were taken with those caches warmed *outside* the timed region,
deliberately. Leaving a five-minute compile inside would put one enormous outlier in exactly
one rep of one variant and nowhere else, which is neither an honest cold number nor an honest
average. It is reported separately instead, which is the only way to report it. Metal's
equivalent is 53.9 ms and needs no cache at all.

## Reproducing

```sh
bench/scripts/gen-artifacts.sh                  # circuits, keys, witnesses
bench/scripts/build-rapidsnark.sh               # baseline, macOS
bench/scripts/build-rapidsnark-linux.sh         # baseline, Linux
bench/aws/provision.sh                          # the NVIDIA box
bench/aws/run-gpu-bench.sh ubuntu@<ip> 10       # sync, build, warm, benchmark, pull
bench/aws/terminate.sh                          # and prove it is gone
python3 bench/scripts/summarize.py --write      # regenerate TABLES.md
```

The proving keys under `bench/artifacts/` come from a locally generated powers of tau, not a
real ceremony. They are benchmark keys and must never be used for anything else. This affects
soundness, not timing or correctness.
